What it is

"Tell me about this project."

An iOS app that puts three music sources into one library, one queue, one search box, and one set of playlists: MP3s you own, Apple Music, and Spotify. A playlist can mix all three. There is also a real-time effects chain — speed, pitch, reverb, EQ, trim — but only for your own files, and that limit is the most interesting thing in the project rather than a shortcoming.

Around it: a Spring Boot API that syncs playlists between devices, a React web client for editing them from a desktop, and Terraform for the AWS it runs on.

One-sentence version

Three music sources that have almost nothing in common, hidden behind one interface that does not pretend they are the same.

The hard part

"What made this technically difficult?"

A local MP3 and an Apple Music track look identical in a list. Underneath, they are not variations on one system — they are two incompatible ones, and Spotify is a third.

Your MP3Apple MusicSpotify
Who plays it Us, AVAudioEngine A system player The Spotify app
Can we read the audio? Yes, every sample No — DRM No — another process
Effects possible? Yes Never Never
Where the audio lives Our process Someone else's Someone else's

Everything awkward in the project comes from one line of that table: for two of the three sources, the audio is not ours. We can ask for it to be played. We cannot touch it.

Say this

"Effects are local-only, and that is structural, not a to-do. Streaming audio is decoded in a process I do not control, so there is no point at which I could apply a filter. The model encodes that as a property of the source rather than leaving it as a comment."

Two protocols, kept apart

"Walk me through your architecture."

This is the centre of the whole design. There are two questions, and they are not the same question:

  • PlaybackProvider — how is this played?
  • MusicSource — where did this track come from?

The obvious move is to merge them into one "Service" type. That is wrong, and knowing why is the answer to the question:

Why they stay separate

They do not line up one-to-one. If your Apple Music subscription lapses you can still browse the catalogue but not play it — browsable, not playable. And a provider can still play a track its source no longer lists.

Merge them and you get one interface that has to lie about at least one of those states.

The payoff was real rather than theoretical. Adding Spotify as a third source touched no existing source and no existing provider — it was two new files implementing two existing protocols. That turns "I designed for extensibility" into "I extended it", which is a much better sentence in an interview.

The rest of the layout

Domain/ Track, Playlist, AudioEdit — no framework imports at all Playback/ PlaybackProvider + the coordinator that owns the queue Library/ MusicSource + local / Apple Music / Spotify, SwiftData Search/ one query fanned out to every source at once Studio/ the effects UI and the offline export UI/ library, playlists, now playing, mini player

Domain/ importing no frameworks is deliberate: those types are pure Swift, so they can be tested without a simulator, and they are what ports to another platform.

What happens when you tap a song

"Walk me through a request." (The mobile version of that question.)

tap a row → coordinator looks at track.source → picks the matching provider → stops whatever was playing → loads and plays → publishes title, art and position to the lock screen

The coordinator is the only object that knows more than one provider exists. Views never choose a player, and providers never know about each other.

Three details worth remembering

Seeking uses a generation counter. When you seek, the engine stops and re-schedules from the new position. The catch: the "finished playing" callback also fires when audio is cancelled — so every seek looked like the track ending, and the app skipped to the next song. A counter bumped on each seek makes stale callbacks identify themselves and get ignored.

Lock screen position is published on transitions only, never on a timer. iOS extrapolates the position itself from elapsed time and a rate you give it. Publish on a timer as well and the two fight, and the lock screen drifts away from what you hear.

Trimmed tracks report trim-relative time. Trim a song to start at 0:30 and it reports 0:00 at its first audible sample. The UI never sees the file's real timeline.

Playlists

"How did you model that?"

A playlist has to hold an MP3, an Apple Music track and a Spotify track in one ordered list. Two obvious approaches both fail:

ApproachWhy not
Database relationship It can only point at local tracks, so playlists become local-only — the exact thing the feature exists to avoid. Relationships are also unordered.
Store the track objects A streaming track is only meaningful while that service answers. Caching someone else's catalogue serves stale copies of their data.

What it actually stores, per entry:

  • the source and that source's id — enough to find the track again anywhere;
  • a snapshot of title, artist and duration.
The snapshot is the part to defend

Without it, a playlist of Apple Music tracks becomes a list of blank rows the moment the subscription lapses or the network drops. A playlist should be able to describe itself without a server's permission.

The cost, stated honestly: entries are stored as one JSON document, so the database cannot answer "which playlists contain this track" without scanning. That is a fair price for ordering and cross-source support, and naming the cost is what makes the answer sound like judgement rather than enthusiasm.

Sync, and why it is not just "save to the server"

"How do you handle the same data on two devices?"

Three pieces, each solving one problem:

1. A dirty flag

Every edited playlist row is marked as having unsynced changes. That flag is in the database, so it survives a crash, a force-quit and a flat battery. The sync itself is scheduled — two seconds after you stop editing, when the app returns to the foreground, and when it leaves the screen — but nothing depends on that timing. A missed sync delays an edit; it cannot lose one, because the queue is the database.

2. Version numbers

Every playlist has a version. The client sends the version it last read; if the server has moved on it refuses the write and returns its own copy. Two devices cannot silently overwrite each other.

When both sides changed, the app reports a conflict and does nothing. Silently picking a winner is how someone loses an evening's work without ever being told.

3. A server epoch

The subtle one, found in production. "The server does not have this playlist, and I last synced it at version 7" normally means it was deleted elsewhere — so the client deletes its copy. Correct, as long as the absence really is a deletion.

Against a database that was replaced or restarted, that same absence means those version numbers were never that server's — and honouring it destroys playlists nobody deleted. So the server publishes one random value that changes when its history does. A client seeing an unfamiliar value re-uploads instead of deleting.

The line to remember

"A version number only means something inside the history that issued it."

The Spotify wall

"Tell me about a mistake." — this is your best answer.

The symptom: playing an MP3 and skipping to a Spotify track threw you out of the app into Spotify. Going the other way was seamless.

What I did wrong: I shipped three confident fixes without taking a single measurement. None could ever have worked. The asymmetry was the clue and I talked past it three times.

What settled it: a twenty-line logger writing to a file on the phone. It showed the connection failing before the app switch, every time — which is what a suspended process looks like from the outside.

The real cause

Spotify audio plays in Spotify's process. While your MP3 plays, Spotify makes no sound, so iOS suspends it. A suspended app cannot accept a connection, and the only way to wake it is to launch it — which iOS permits only from the foreground.

There is no entitlement that keeps another app alive. This is a platform constraint, not a bug.

What shipped instead of a fix: a transition screen so the switch looks intentional; and from the lock screen, where waking another app is not permitted at all, the queue skips to the next track it can play and posts a silent notification saying what it passed over. Stopping the music and asking you to unlock was built first and rejected on use — the person not looking at their phone is exactly the one who cannot act on the request.

The lesson, in one sentence

Reasoning about another process's lifecycle from inside your own is guessing, however good the guess sounds — and the logger that would have saved three attempts cost twenty minutes.

The rest of the stack, briefly

"Is this just an iOS app?"

PieceWhat and why
APISpring Boot, Java 21, Postgres. Playlist sync, accounts, conflict handling.
WebReact + TypeScript. A control plane, not a player — browsers cannot play any of these sources, and the UI says so instead of showing a dead play button.
AuthCognito, with OAuth2 + PKCE from both clients. No AWS SDK on the phone — it is two HTTP calls, and the same flow runs on web.
InfraTerraform: Cognito, RDS, ECR, ECS, CloudFront. The API runs on ECS Express Mode behind a managed load balancer.
CIGitHub Actions, federated into AWS by OIDC — no AWS key is stored anywhere, so there is nothing to leak.

The cost decision worth telling

Containers that need to pull an image and reach the internet normally sit in a private network behind a NAT gateway — about $32/month. Instead, the API tasks run in public subnets with public addresses and a security group that admits only the load balancer. Addressable, but not reachable. The database stays private.

The saving is not the point. The reasoning is: enumerate what genuinely needs to leave the network, then buy only that, instead of reaching for the general-purpose default.

Five questions you will get

Read this section last, the night before.

Why not React Native, so you get Android for free?

Apple Music has no playback SDK on Android, so "cross-platform" was never going to deliver real Apple Music playback there. And the effects chain — the most interesting part — would have meant bridging native audio anyway. Going native removed the bridge.

Why can't the effects work on streaming?

Effects are an audio graph applied to samples. Streaming audio is decrypted and decoded inside another process that hands out no samples — only "play", "pause", "seek". There is no point in the pipeline where a filter could sit. It is a property of the source, and the code says so.

Why ECS Express Mode instead of full ECS or Lambda?

Full ECS means hand-wiring a network, a load balancer and task definitions — days that do not buy proportional signal with two clients still to build. Lambda was rejected because Spring Boot cold starts there are genuinely bad without extra build complexity. I picked App Runner first for exactly this reason; AWS then closed it to new services, and Express Mode is the same bargain on ECS. The move cost an afternoon and a network change. The signal is the infrastructure being in Terraform, and being able to say: managed runtime on purpose, here is what it cost when the platform moved, and here is when I would go further.

What would you do differently?

Measure before fixing. The Spotify handoff cost three wrong fixes because I reasoned from the outside instead of instrumenting. And I would add database migrations from the first deploy rather than the second — the production config validated the schema and nothing created it, so the service crash-looped against an empty database until I added Flyway — and then the deploy target itself disappeared under me when AWS closed App Runner, which is its own lesson about not treating a managed service as permanent.

What is not finished?

The web client cannot play audio and does not offer to — browsers can play none of the three sources, so it is a control plane by design. Say that plainly; being precise about what a piece is for is worth more than claiming more.
